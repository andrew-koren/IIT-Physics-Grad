## Introduction

Observations of surface magnetic fields in stars are explained by either the dynamo hypothesis (fields are generated by convection) or the fossil hypothesis (field is a remnant from an earlier evolutionary phase). The fossil hypothesis is attractive for massive main-sequence stars which have convective cores but radiative envelopes. It also appears relevant to red giant branch stars, where asteroseismic observations indicate strong core fields and the current radiative core was convective on the main sequence. 

A long-lived fossil field requires a stable magnetic equilibrium, and the Prendergast field has long been proposed as such a candidate. Previous analytic work by Prendergast (1958) found that the $$m=0$$ mode could be unstable only if the magnetic energy exceeded 0.4 times the gravitational binding energy, while Cowling (1960) suggested instability in neutrally stratified stars for all field strengths. More recent nonlinear simulations by Duez et al. (2010) and Braithwaite & Nordlund (2006) saw no evidence of instability on short timescales, leading to the widespread assumption that the Prendergast field is dynamically stable. 

In this paper the authors demonstrate that the Prendergast field is in fact linearly unstable under all conditions tested, including strong stable stratification and different boundary conditions, and that the instability operates on a timescale faster than stellar evolution.

## Numerical Details

The authors solve the linearized magnetohydrodynamic equations in the Boussinesq approximation using the Dedalus pseudospectral code in spherical coordinates. Lengths are scaled by the stellar core radius $$R$$, time by the Alfvén time $$t_A = R/v_A$$ with $$v_A = B_0/\sqrt{4\pi\rho_c}$$, and the magnetic field by the maximum of $$|B_0|$$. 

The background equilibrium is the Prendergast field given by equations (7)–(9), with the lowest-energy non-singular solution corresponding to $$\lambda \approx 5.76346$$ and amplitude $$\beta \approx 1.31765$$. This field vanishes at the outer boundary $$r = 1$$. An initial velocity perturbation of prescribed azimuthal wavenumber $$m$$ 
is seeded via a profile $$ \mathbf{u}_0 = \sin(\theta)^m \sin(m\phi) e^{-(r-0.875)^2/(0.02)^2} \mathbf{e}_r $$
, after which divergence cleaning yields an incompressible initial flow. 

Two magnetic boundary conditions are used: potential-field (POT) and perfectly-conducting (PC). POT yields a current-free and continuous external field, enforced by $$ \partial_r A_\ell + (\ell+1)A_\ell/r = 0 $$. PC 
with no normal magnetic field and constant electric potential, i.e., $$\mathbf{e}_\theta\cdot\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{e}_\phi\cdot\mathbf{A} = \Phi = 0$$. 

All boundaries are stress-free, impenetrable, and have zero density perturbation at $$r=1$$. Magnetic field lines and velocity flows are visualized with the VAPOR toolkit, seeding field lines from a grid of points and integrating to reveal the three-dimensional structure of the eigenmodes.

## Results

The Prendergast magnetic field exhibits a linear instability: kinetic energy grows exponentially after an initial decay. The growth is robust to increased resolution and smaller timestep, confirming a physical (non-numerical) origin. The unstable eigenmode has an $$m=1$$ structure with flipped symmetry axis compared to the background field.

Growth rate decreases as resistivity decreases, following a power‑law relation $$\gamma \propto \eta^\beta$$. The instability is thus resistive in nature and lowering diffusivity slows it down but does not eliminate it.

Stable stratification reduces the growth rate at low $$N_0^2$$, but above a threshold $$\gamma$$ becomes roughly constant, independent of stratification strength. Radial velocities are strongly suppressed at high $$N_0^2$$, yet radial magnetic energy and growth rate remain nearly unchanged because azimuthal flows sustain the field. The instability is expected to persist even at stellar stratification levels.

Only $$m = 0$$ and $$m = 1$$ modes are unstable, as higher $$m$$ decay. Both potential (POT) and perfectly conducting (PC) boundary conditions yield instability, though POT gives larger growth rates. The power‑law index of $$\gamma(\eta)$$ differs between boundary conditions.

The $$m=1$$ mode shows evidence of a tearing‑mode‑like resistive instability at the origin (guide‑field point). The normal magnetic field perturbation is continuous, but its gradient sharpens with decreasing resistivity, suggesting a discontinuous derivative in the $$\eta\!\to\!0$$ limit. However, the scaling exponent is not exactly 3/5, so the mechanism is a combination of effects. The $$m=0$$ mode cannot exhibit tearing behavior due to axisymmetry, indicating other destabilizing processes also operate.

Using the power‑law scaling $$\gamma \propto (\eta/\nu_A R)^\beta \nu_A/R$$, the e‑folding time for a typical red giant core is estimated as $$\sim 1.2\times 10^6$$ years, much shorter than the RGB evolution timescale of a few million years. Therefore the Prendergast field is not a viable stable magnetic field configuration in stellar interiors.

## Conclusion
The Prendergast magnetic field is linearly unstable on timescales longer than those previously studied, in the form of a physical and resistive instability.
Growth rate decreases with resistivity, remains present under strong stable stratification, and is sensitive to boundary conditions and azimuthal wavenumber ($$m=0,1$$ only, other modes decay).
The $$m=1$$ instability shows tearing‑mode‑like features, but tearing is not the sole mechanism.
Extrapolation to stellar conditions yields an e‑folding time far shorter than the stellar lifetime, implying the Prendergast field is unsuitable as a model for stable fossil fields in stars.
The nonlinear saturation of the instability and the search for truly stable magnetic equilibria require future work.
